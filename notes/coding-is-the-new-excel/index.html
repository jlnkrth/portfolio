Principle

Coding Is the New Excel

My head of content opened a pull request this morning. Coding isn’t hard anymore — and it’s becoming part of the job, the way spreadsheets did.

This morning my head of content Slack’d me: please make sure I merge the PR she opened, so the latest update to our content pipeline goes live. She didn’t ask me to write the change. She wrote it. She opened the pull request. My job was the merge button.

A few years ago that message would have been weird. Content people didn’t open PRs. They filed tickets and waited for an engineer. Today it felt normal, and that’s the whole point of this piece. Coding isn’t hard the way it used to be. And for a growing share of jobs, working with AI (creating skills, sharing them, maintaining them) is becoming a requirement the way Excel became a requirement: you don’t need to know how the spreadsheet engine works. You need to know how to use it.

The message that shouldn’t be strange

What she actually shipped wasn’t a React rewrite. It was an update to how our content pipeline behaves: the prompts, the checks, the handoff rules. In our world that lives next to the code, in pull requests, because the agent that runs the pipeline reads the same files the team edits. So when the pipeline needs a change, the person who owns the content opens the PR.

I used to be the person who translated her “can we make it do X?” into a commit. That translation step was the expensive part, and it was also the slow part. Now she goes straight to the file. Claude Code helps write the diff. She reviews whether it matches the judgment she already has. I review whether it’s safe to ship. The loop got shorter because the domain expert stopped needing a translator.

The domain expert moved one column left. That’s the whole shift.

You never needed to know how Excel works

Think about what “knowing Excel” meant at work for the last twenty years. Almost nobody on a marketing or ops team could explain VLOOKUP’s internal evaluation order. Almost everybody could open a sheet, build a model, share it, and keep it honest when the numbers drifted. The people who couldn’t use Excel got stuck waiting on the people who could.

Nobody called that “becoming a software engineer.” It was just literacy for knowledge work. Spreadsheets became the place where judgment got stored in a form other people could run. That is exactly what skills are becoming for AI agents. A skill is a checklist the machine can follow. Sharing a skill is sharing the spreadsheet. Maintaining a skill is fixing the formula when the world changes.

build a sheet[ create a skill ]
send the file to a teammate[ ship it in a plugin or PR ]
fix the formula next quarter[ maintain the skill when output drifts ]
don’t recompile Excel[ don’t become a systems engineer ]

Same literacy, new surface. The hard part was never the tool.

So when I say coding is becoming a job requirement, I don’t mean CS degrees and leetcode. I mean: can you sit with an AI, turn how you do the work into something reusable, get it into the shared toolkit, and keep it true over time? That’s the Excel bar. My head of content cleared it this morning.

Skills are where the job lives now

Working with AI, at the useful level, is mostly a loop that looks boring on paper: create skills that capture how a job should be done, share them so the rest of the team (and the agents) run the same version, and maintain them when reality changes, which it always does.

Create is the writing-down part. The SOP that used to live in someone’s head becomes a skill file the agent loads. Share is the distribution part: a pull request, a Claude Code plugin, a folder the whole company installs. Maintain is the part people skip, and it’s the part that decides whether the machine stays useful. A skill you never revisit becomes a spreadsheet with a broken exchange rate still hard-coded from 2019. 

When a skill needs an update

1. Notice the drift (bad output, new edge case, new client rule).
2. Edit the skill yourself — don't file a ticket for a prompt change.
3. Open a PR so the change is reviewable and versioned.
4. Merge, then watch the next few runs.
5. If it still drifts, the skill is wrong, not the model.

This is the loop my head of content ran. The Slack ping was step 4.

Honest limit: some changes still need an engineer. Auth, billing, anything that can delete production data. The content pipeline update this morning wasn’t that. Most of the work that used to wait in the engineering queue also isn’t that. The queue was full of judgment that only the domain expert had, waiting for someone who could type it into a file.

Teach the machine, then manage it

Here’s the sentence I keep coming back to. If you can teach a machine how to do a job, your job becomes managing the machine. Teaching is encoding your judgment into skills and workflows. Managing is watching the runs, catching drift, deciding when to escalate to a human, and shipping the next patch to the skill.

That sounds like a demotion if you picture management as meetings. It isn’t. Managing the machine is closer to editing: you’re still the taste. You’re just applying taste to the process instead of to every single draft. The people who refuse to teach the machine keep doing every unit by hand. The people who teach it get their time back, and then their scarce skill is keeping the machine honest.

Which is why the Slack message matters as a signal, not as a cute anecdote. The content lead didn’t ask for permission to have an opinion about the pipeline. She updated the pipeline. Coding got easy enough that the opinion and the change could be the same person. That’s the new requirement for the job.

You don’t need to know how Excel works. You need to open the sheet, keep the formulas true, and share the file. Same deal now, with skills and agents. Teach the machine how you do the work. Then your job is managing the machine.
